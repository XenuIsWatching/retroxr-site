Not everything RetroXR runs is a console. Point-and-click adventures, DOS games, Amigas,
STs and home computers all want a **pointer** and a **keyboard**, and both are first-class
here.

## Two ways to point

### The mouse already on your desk

The most comfortable option, and the one in the clip above. Spawn a **mouse receiver**,
plug it into the machine's port, and the mouse next to your keyboard drives the game —
with nothing held in either hand.

<Turntable
	src="/video/receivers/mouse_receiver.mp4"
	poster={mousePoster}
	alt="The mouse receiver rotating: a blue box with mouse and Bluetooth glyphs on its top face."
	caption="The mouse receiver. Seat it in a port and your real mouse is the game's mouse."
/>

Movement is **relative**, exactly as a real mouse is: the game gets how far you moved, not
where you are. That is why the cursor behaves the way it always has, including in games
that hide the pointer and read raw motion.

### The virtual mouse

<Turntable
	src="/video/input/retro_mouse.mp4"
	poster={retroMousePoster}
	alt="RetroXR's mouse rotating: a simple two-button mouse with a cable attachment at the back."
	caption="The mouse you can pick up. It plugs into a controller port with the same cable and plug a controller uses."
/>

There is also a mouse you can pick up and slide across a surface in VR. It is a lovely
object, and a poor pointing device — it needs a hand, a flat surface and your attention.
It exists because it is *right*, not because it is the fastest way to play a LucasArts
game.

Use it when you want the room to feel real. Use the receiver when you want to finish
Monkey Island.

#### It sticks to the surface

The thing that makes it usable at all: when the mouse comes near a surface it **glues to
that surface's plane**. What you see is your hand's position projected flat onto the desk,
so small lifts and the natural wobble of holding your arm out neither show nor move the
cursor.

Pull your hand far enough away and it lets go — the mouse snaps back to where your hand
actually is, and stops reporting motion until it lands again.

That is why sliding it across a desk feels like sliding a mouse rather than waving a brick
in the air.

| What you do | What the mouse does |
| --- | --- |
| **Trigger** | Left button |
| **A / X button** | Right button |
| **B / Y button** | Middle button |
| Slide it on a surface | Moves the cursor |
| Lift it clear | Stops reporting until it lands again |

## Typing

<Turntable
	src="/video/receivers/keyboard_receiver.mp4"
	poster={kbPoster}
	alt="The keyboard receiver rotating: a tan box with keyboard and Bluetooth glyphs on its top face."
	caption="The keyboard receiver. Seat it and type — no capture key, no mode."
/>

Computers need a keyboard, and there are two of those too.

**The receiver** forwards your real keyboard to the port it is plugged into. Seated is
live; there is no capture key and no mode to remember, because plugging something in is
not an ambiguous act.

**The virtual keyboard** is a board you can pick up and hold. Because holding a keyboard
*is* ambiguous — you might merely be carrying it — your typing does not reach the game
until you press **Scroll Lock**. That is deliberate, and it is why the receiver is easier
if you are going to be typing for a while.

<Turntable
	src="/video/input/retro_keyboard.mp4"
	poster={retroKeyboardPoster}
	alt="RetroXR's keyboard rotating: a full-size keyboard with individually modeled keys."
	caption="The keyboard. Every key is its own modeled cap, and every one can be pressed individually."
/>

#### Every key is a real key

The board is not a picture of a keyboard. **Each cap is its own object, and each one can be
pressed on its own** — reach out and poke a key with a fingertip, or point at it and press.

That means you can type on it in VR without a physical keyboard anywhere near you, which
is the only option for a computer system if you are standing in the middle of a room with
nothing to rest a real board on.

For anything longer than a password, use the receiver and your own keyboard.

## Setting it up

<Steps>

1. Connect your mouse and keyboard to the headset or PC as you normally would.

2. Open `SPAWN` → **Controllers**. Receivers appear **only once their device is
   connected** — if you cannot see one, that is why.

3. Spawn the receiver you want and push its plug into the machine's port.

4. Play. The LED on the box shows it is live.

</Steps>

Full detail on [using a real gamepad, keyboard or
mouse](/guide/playing/external-devices/).

## Which systems want this

- **ScummVM** — the point-and-click adventures. Mouse essential, keyboard occasionally.
- **DOS** — mouse and keyboard both.
- **Amiga, Atari ST, C64, MSX, PC-98** — keyboard essential.
- **Consoles with a mouse peripheral**, like the SNES mouse.

For consoles, the mouse is a *peripheral* you plug into a controller port rather than a
system input — see [controllers and peripherals](/guide/playing/controllers/).

<Aside type="note" title="On desktop, they are already yours">
	Receivers are a VR idea. Running RetroXR on a PC without a headset, your keyboard and
	mouse are how you walk around the room — so the virtual keyboard keeps its Scroll Lock
	capture and mouse-look is never taken away from you. See
	[desktop controls](/guide/reference/desktop-controls/).
</Aside>
