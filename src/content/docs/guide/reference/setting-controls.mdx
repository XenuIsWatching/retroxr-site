Everything that presses a button in RetroXR is rebindable, and it all lives under the
**`CONTROLS`** tab.

<Image src={controlsTab} alt="The CONTROLS tab, listing gamepad buttons against their bound keys — D-pad up, down, left and right on W, S, A and D, and the face buttons on the numeric keypad." />

Each row is one control on the emulated machine, with whatever currently drives it beside
it. Change a row and it takes effect immediately.

## Three kinds of input, one screen

| What you are binding | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- |
| **VR controllers** | The Quest controllers in your hands |
| **Keyboard** | The real keyboard, on desktop or through a receiver |
| **A real gamepad** | Anything connected over Bluetooth or USB |

They are not exclusive. A control can have a VR binding and a key at the same time, and
whichever you press wins — pressing one never cancels the other.

## Global, or per system

Bindings resolve in layers:

1. **Defaults** — what RetroXR ships with.
2. **Your global bindings** — applied everywhere.
3. **Per-system bindings** — applied only to that machine.

The most specific one wins. So you can set a layout you like everywhere, and still give
one console its own arrangement without disturbing the rest.

As of v0.4.0 this applies to **both** the XR controllers and any external gamepad — each
platform can carry its own map for either, set from the `CONTROLS` menu.

<Aside type="note" title="A per-system map is a whole map">
	Giving a platform its own layout takes a complete copy of your bindings at that moment,
	not a patch over the global ones. Change a global binding afterwards and that platform
	keeps what it had — which is the point, but it does mean a platform you have customised
	will not pick up later global edits. Clear its map and it goes back to following global.
</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="Why per-system exists">
	Machines disagree about what their buttons are called. A layout that is comfortable on
	a two-button NES pad is not the same as one for a six-button arcade stick, and neither
	suits a Wii Remote. Per-system bindings are how you stop tuning one and breaking
	another.
</Aside>

## Devices with their own layer

Some hardware has bindings of its own, because it is not shaped like a joypad:

- **Wii Remote** — its own map, since it has seven buttons, a d-pad and motion. See
  [the Wii page](/guide/platforms/wii/#controls).
- **Nunchuk** — a small map of its own, chained behind the remote, motion included.
- **Light gun** — its own trigger and buttons.

These read from the same file and follow the same global-then-per-system rule.

## Using real hardware

A real gamepad, keyboard or mouse reaches a machine through a **receiver** — a small box
you plug into a controller port, rather than anything you configure in a menu.

## Desktop mode

With no headset, the keyboard is both how you walk around the room *and* how you play. The
full default map is on [desktop controls](/guide/reference/desktop-controls/).

The two do not collide, because a held keyboard only hijacks your typing once **Scroll
Lock** capture is on. Off, the keys still belong to you.

## Comfort settings are elsewhere

Turning style, snap angle, the movement vignette, height and world scale are not bindings
— they are under `OPTIONS`, and covered on [graphics and
comfort](/guide/reference/graphics-and-comfort/).

:::caution[Needs an in-headset pass]
The layering model and the per-device maps are read from the bindings source and are
accurate. The exact controls on the `CONTROLS` tab — how a row is rebound, and whether
per-system is a toggle or a separate page — have not been checked against a running build.
:::
